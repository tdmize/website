7.2 Testing for Interaction: Second Differences

  • With nonlinear models, you cannot rely on the coefficient on the interaction (product) term as a test of interaction/moderation
    • Consider the β coefficient a nuisance parameter: you need to estimate/include it to obtain accurate predictions—but you are not interested in that β coefficient estimate in and of itself
    • In a nonlinear model, the coefficient on the interaction (product) term is not a test of interaction in the natural metric of the DV (e.g., predicted probability in logit/probit)
  • You must test for the interaction effect in terms of the second difference in the effect on the predictions. Note:
    • 1st difference = marginal effect (ME)
    • 2nd difference = difference in MEs
      • The overall spirit of my recommendation is test what your hypothesis predicts in the metric of interest; do not assume the coefficients are in this metric
      • See Mize 2019 for all the details

Misinterpreting the interaction coefficient

  • Most people get this wrong and assume the coefficient on the interaction (product) terms tests the interaction in the predictions:
    • “A review of 13 economics journals listed on JSTOR found 72 articles published between 1980 and 1999 that used interaction terms in nonlinear models. None of the studies interpreted the coefficient on the interaction term correctly.” (Ai and Norton 2003 p.123)
    • “… I examined the 53 articles published in the American Sociological Review (ASR) between 2004 and 2016 examining nonlinear interaction effects. Fifty of the 53 referred only to the coefficient on the product term to determine the significance of the interaction effect—an improper test of interaction in terms of the predicted probabilities.” (Mize 2019 p.82)
  • Plots below from Mize (2019) demonstrate how discrepant the effect on the predictions can be from the size and statistical significance of coefficient on the interaction (product) term

Motivating example: Perceptions of love and support for formal rights and informal privileges for couples

  • Example from Doan, Loehr, and Miller 2015 SF. Nationally representative survey experiment (vignette) describing a couple

Brian and Jennifer met three years ago and were immediately attracted to each other. After going on a few dates, Brian told Jennifer that he wanted to see her exclusively, to which Jennifer happily agreed; they’ve been together ever since. Although Brian and Jennifer have had serious arguments, they both report being happy when they are together. Brian and Jennifer feel complete trust in each other. In fact, they’ve lived together for the past two years.

Motivating example: couple type X love

  • Focal IV:
    • How in love do you think this couple is? (0 to 10 scale)
  • Moderating IV: Vignette random assigned couple type (sexuality) based on names:
    • Heterosexual couple; Lesbian couple; Gay couple
  • DVs:
    • Continuous scale: Are you supportive of informal privileges for this couple (e.g., talk about relationship with others; hold hands in public; kiss each other on cheek; etc.)?
    • Binary: Do you think they should be allowed to legally marry?

This tells us that increases in perceptions of love from 4 to 8 have significant effects for both heterosexual and gay couples. But are these effects different?

  • A second difference is a test of whether two first differences are equal or not. Here, we have:
    • An effect (first difference) of love for heterosexual couples
    • An effect (first difference) of love for gay couples
      • Second difference = if effect is different for heterosexual and gay couples
  • From above, we know:
    • 1st difference for heterosexual couples = 1.745
    • 1st difference for gay couple = 2.830
      • 2nd difference = 2.830 – 1.745 = 1.085
