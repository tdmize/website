7.5 Continuous X Continuous Interactions

  • Interactions with two continuous variables are the most difficult to interpret and present. However, there are visual tools that greatly simplify interpretation
    • Figures are always necessary to convey the needed information. Three options:
      1. Choose “ideal types” of one continuous IV and plot as you would for a continuous * nominal interaction
      2. Plot marginal effect of one continuous IV over the range of the other IV
        • Separately, for both IVs
      3. Three-dimensions in 2D via contour plots
  • Following examples from GSS 1973 – 2024:
    • Pr(Think Same-Sex Relationships are Acceptable) tracked over time, and based on political views + controls

Ideal types

  • Earlier in the class, we defined ideal types by clusters of multiple IVs
  • Can also think of ideal typical values for specific IVs
  • E.g., Say you were in charge of casting for a movie and the script calls for a “young person”, a “middle aged person”, and an “older person”
    • What age would you target for each role?
      • This is your ideal type / prototypical value

Limitations of ideal type approach; tests of 2nd difference

  • “Although I find [the ideal type figure] a clear, intuitive, and helpful way to summarize these effects, it is also important to substantiate these intuitions with additional analyses and tests of statistical significance.” (Mize 2019 p.108-109)
  • The interaction effect is not directly tested in the ideal type figure
    • Always provide a statistical test for the interaction as well as the figure
  • If choosing substantive ideal types, test the ME of the focal IV across those values
    • 2nd difference is test of interaction
  • Absent theoretically informed values, I generally test the ME of the focal IV at:
    • The 5th (or 10th) percentile as a “low” ideal type*
    • The 50th percentile (median) as an “average” ideal type
    • The 95th (or 90th) percentile as a “high” ideal type*
      • And then all 2nd differences across these three ideal types

*I generally like 5th and 95th to represent truly low and high values; but with small samples or highly skewed distributions, I would use 10th and 90th percentiles

Marginal effects plots

  • Marginal effects plots are an effective way to present a more direct test of the interaction
  • Plot the ME of one continuous IV across the range of the other IV
    • A flat line suggests no interaction: size of ME of focal IV is similar across range of moderating IV
    • Non-flat lines suggest interaction: size of ME of focal IV differs across range
  • Can make two plots; one for each IV’s ME
    • But, sometimes strong theory to just show ME of focal IV across (trimmed) range of moderating IV

  • Stata
  • R

Figure # illustrates that conservative political views are consistently associated with less support for same-sex relationships, across all years of the GSS. But there is variation over time: the effect of political views in more recent years is much stronger than the effect of political views in the earliest surveys in the 1970s. For illustration, consider the earliest and latest year in the GSS: in 1973 a standard deviation increase in conservatism predicted only a 3 percentage point decline in support for same-sex relationships; that same +SD change in 2024 predicts a 15 percentage point decline (2nd difference = -0.116, p < .001).

Contour plots

  • One of the primary difficulties of interpreting continuous X continuous interactions is thinking in two dimensions
  • Contour plots present the Pr(y = 1) at the intersection of the two continuous variables included in the interaction
    • Each IV makes up an axis (one on x; one on y)
    • Color is used to present the information on the Pr(y = 1)
  • These work best when you can use color so you can easily distinguish the different Pr(y = 1)
    • So, works well in a talk or a journal that allows color

*These plots are made in a different way in Stata than other plots I’ve shown. See Appendix A7.5 for the full details

Contour plots in Stata and R

  • Stata
  • R
  • In Stata, use margins to make predictions at all combinations across the (trimmed) ranges of both continuous IVs
    • Make the plot* using twoway contour
      • Syntax: (1) variable with the predictions comes first, (2) followed by the IV you want on the x-axis, then (3) the IV you want on the y-axis

*See Appendix A7.5 for the full Stata details

  • In R, save predictions for all combinations of the continuous IVs
    • Use ggplot and geom_contour_filled
      • Syntax: (1) IV you want on the x-axis, (2) the IV you want on the y-axis, then (3) z dimension is the saved estimate of Pr(y=1)
# 7.5.d - 7.5.h: Contour plots
# Make predictions at all combinations of both continuous IVs (one year at a time,
# which needs much less memory than one call for all 676 combinations)
pr4 <- bind_rows(lapply(1:52, function(y) {
  avg_predictions(samemod, by = c("conviewSS", "year1972"), vcov = FALSE,
    newdata = datagrid(conviewSS = seq(1, 7, .5), year1972 = y,
      grid_type = "counterfactual"))
}))

# 7.5.g - Ordinal color scheme viridis (the default)
ggplot(pr4, aes(x = year1972, y = conviewSS, z = estimate)) +
  geom_contour_filled(breaks = seq(0, 1, .05)) +
  scale_fill_viridis_d(direction = -1, drop = FALSE) +
  scale_x_continuous(breaks = c(2, 8, 18, 28, 38, 48),
    labels = c(1974, 1980, 1990, 2000, 2010, 2020), expand = c(0, 0)) +

    "Liberal", "Middle", "Conservative", "Very\nconservative"), expand = c(0, 0)) +
