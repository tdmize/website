5.3 Coefficients in the Ordinal Model

  • An alternative way to interpret the ordinal model is with cumulative probabilities. I don’t find these as helpful as predicted probabilities, but the basic idea is important to understand for odds ratios interpretations (see Long and Freese 2014 for more on cumulative probability interpretations and plots)

Cumulative probabilities in ordinal logit

  • The cumulative probability equals:

\[\Pr(y \leq j\,|\,\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{m = 1}^{j}\Pr(y = m\,|\,\mathbf{x})\]

For example

\[\begin{aligned} \Pr(SD\,|\,\mathbf{x}) &= \Pr(y = 1\,|\,\mathbf{x}) \\ \Pr(SD + D\,|\,\mathbf{x}) &= \Pr(y = 1\,|\,\mathbf{x}) + \Pr(y = 2\,|\,\mathbf{x}) \\ \Pr(SD + D + A\,|\,\mathbf{x}) &= \Pr(y = 1\,|\,\mathbf{x}) + \Pr(y = 2\,|\,\mathbf{x}) + \Pr(y = 3\,|\,\mathbf{x}) \end{aligned}\]

  • For SD + D + A

\[\begin{aligned} \Pr(SD + D + A\,|\,\mathbf{x}) &= \Pr(y \leq 3) \\ &= \Pr(y = 1\,|\,\mathbf{x}) + \Pr(y = 2\,|\,\mathbf{x}) + \Pr(y = 3\,|\,\mathbf{x}) \\ &= [\Lambda(\tau_{1} - \mathbf{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{\beta}) - \Lambda(\tau_{0} - \mathbf{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{\beta})] + [\Lambda(\tau_{2} - \mathbf{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{\beta}) - \Lambda(\tau_{1} - \mathbf{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{\beta})] \\ &\quad + [\Lambda(\tau_{3} - \mathbf{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{\beta}) - \Lambda(\tau_{2} - \mathbf{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{\beta})] \\ &= [\Lambda(\tau_{3} - \mathbf{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{\beta}) - \Lambda(\tau_{0} - \mathbf{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{\beta})] = \Lambda(\tau_{3} - \mathbf{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{\beta}) \end{aligned}\]

\[\Pr(y \leq j\,|\,\mathbf{x}) = \Lambda(\tau_{j} - \mathbf{x}\boldsymbol{\beta})\]

Cumulative probabilities in ordinal logit

  • This is a binary model with intercept \(\alpha_{j} = (\tau_{j} - \beta_{0})\) and slopes \(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*} = -\boldsymbol{\beta}\)

\[\Pr(y \leq j\,|\,\mathbf{x}) = \Lambda(\tau_{j} - \mathbf{x}\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \Lambda(\alpha_{j} - \mathbf{x}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})\]

  • The odds of y ≤ j versus y > j given x are

\[\Omega_{j}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\Pr(y \leq j\,|\,\mathbf{x})}{\Pr(y > j\,|\,\mathbf{x})} = \exp(\tau_{j} - \mathbf{x}\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \exp(\alpha_{j} - \mathbf{x}\boldsymbol{\beta})\]

  • The odds ratio for a change in xk

\[\frac{\Omega_{j}(\mathbf{x}, x_{k} + 1)}{\Omega_{j}(\mathbf{x}, x_{k})} = \exp(\beta_{k}^{*})\]

Interpretation:

For a unit increase in xk the odds of higher outcomes compared to lower outcomes change by a factor of \(\exp(\beta_{k}^{*})\), holding other variables constant

Odds ratios interpretations in ordinal models

  • The odds are identical regardless where you binarize the comparison:

\[\Omega\left(\frac{P(SD)}{P(D, A, SA)}\right) = \Omega\left(\frac{P(SD, D)}{P(A, SA)}\right) = \Omega\left(\frac{P(SD, D, A)}{P(SA)}\right)\]

  • The odds ratio is interpreted in terms of the underlying latent variable (y*)
  • Effective interpretations are contingent on being able to properly and clearly name and talk about this underlying latent variable
    • Remember this must be a single unidirectional latent concept to satisfy the assumptions of the model
  • If you cannot easily or effectively decide what the latent variable is this is a pretty good indicator an ordinal model might not be appropriate

Naming the latent variable y*

  • How would you name and talk about the latent variable y* if you used one of the following ordinal variables as a DV?
    • All examples from the General Social Survey (GSS) which mostly consists of questions asked as ordinal variables

Would you say your own health, in general, is excellent, good, fair, or poor?

How often do you read the newspaper — every day, a few times a week, once a week, less than once a week, or never?

Because of past discrimination, employers should make special efforts to hire and promote qualified women…please tell me whether you strongly agree, agree, neither agree nor disagree, disagree, or strongly disagree.

Methods of birth control should be available to teenagers between the ages of 14 and 16 even if their parents do not approve… strongly agree, agree, disagree, or strongly disagree.

  • Good practice is to code your dependent variable in a way that will aid interpretation: higher numbers should = more of the latent construct

Each year since this question was first asked, the odds of being more supportive has increased by a factor of 1.006

Interpretation tips: As with binary models

  • Need to make sure to use word “odds” in interpretation so metric is clear
  • Need to indicate effect is multiplicative with “times” or “by a factor”, or similar

Pros and cons of ORs in ordinal models

  • ORs have some appealing properties in the ordinal model
    • There is only one for each IV
      • Fewer than the number of MEs to examine
    • The effect is the same across the data space—i.e., you can just say “holding all else constant”
      • MEs will vary based on where in the data you calculate them as Pr(y = j) is nonlinear
    • If you are interested in the latent variable itself, ORs are in this metric
  • Limitations of ORs
    • There is no information about which categories are more or less affected*
      • Even if the OR is significant this does not necessarily mean that a ME for each outcome category will be significant
    • The substantive size of effects are not clear from the OR
    • You cannot compare ORs across models due to rescaling
    • The effects are multiplicative; not additive

*Note that when you estimate a linear regression for an ordinal outcome your β estimates are similarly in terms of the latent variable, but with the additional (usually unreasonable) assumption of meaningful and equidistance spacing between categories

Standardized coefficients

  • A brief primer (or reminder) of what standardized coefficients are in the linear regression context
  • Standardized coefficients convert the metric of the coefficient (β) into standard deviation units
    • We can x-standardize so the coefficient is in terms of a standard deviation increase in x
    • We can y-standardize so the coefficient is in terms of a standard deviation of y
    • We can fully (xy) standardize (apply both x and y standardization)
  • Note that we only x-standardize continuous IVs
    • The SD of a binary/nominal IV is solely a function of the mean, so not meaningful

Standardized coefficients

  • To x-standardize:

\[y = \beta_{0} + (\sigma_{1}\beta_{1})\frac{x_{1}}{\sigma_{1}} + (\sigma_{2}\beta_{2})\frac{x_{2}}{\sigma_{2}} + \varepsilon\]

  • Interpretation: For a standard deviation increase in x, y is expected to change by β

  • To y-standardize:

\[\frac{y}{\sigma_{y}} = \frac{\beta_{0}}{\sigma_{y}} + \frac{\beta_{1}}{\sigma_{y}}x_{1} + \frac{\beta_{2}}{\sigma_{y}}x_{2} + \frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma_{y}}\]

  • Interpretation: For a one-unit increase in x, y is expected to change by β standard deviations

  • To xy AKA fully-standardize:

\[\frac{y}{\sigma_{y}} = \frac{\beta_{0}}{\sigma_{y}} + \left(\frac{\sigma_{1}\beta_{1}}{\sigma_{y}}\right)\frac{x_{1}}{\sigma_{1}} + \left(\frac{\sigma_{2}\beta_{2}}{\sigma_{y}}\right)\frac{x_{2}}{\sigma_{2}} + \frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma_{y}}\]

  • Interpretation: For a standard deviation increase in x, y is expected to change by β standard deviations

Interpreting ordinal models with y* standardization

  • With ordinal models, a y*-standardized interpretation can often be a satisfactory alternative to linear regression if your interest is primarily in interpreting effects on the underlying latent variable y*
    • You also have the ability to examine the probability of specific categories
  • In the ordinal model,

\[y^{*} = \mathbf{x}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \varepsilon = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x_{1} + \cdots + \beta_{K}x_{K} + \varepsilon\]

  • The scale of y* cannot be estimated, so we use y*-standardized coefficients
  • The y* standardized coefficient simply divides β by the standard deviation of y*:

\[\beta_{k}^{Sy^{*}} = \frac{\beta_{k}}{\sigma_{y^{*}}}\]

Interpretation: for a unit increase in \(x_{k}\), \(y^{*}\) is expected to increase by \(\beta_{k}^{Sy^{*}}\) standard deviations holding other variables constant

y* standardization of the coefficients

  • To y* standardize the coefficients we need to calculate the variance of y*

\[{\widehat{\sigma}}_{y^{*}}^{2} = \widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}'\widehat{Var}(\mathbf{x})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} + Var(\varepsilon)\]

  • Where the variance of the errors [\(Var(\varepsilon)\)] is assumed to be 1 in probit; \(\pi^{2}/3\) in logit
  • The \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}'\widehat{Var}(\mathbf{x})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) component* represents the variance in the predicted y* metric
    • We could calculate this by making predictions for everyone in the sample in the \(\mathbf{x}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) metric and then calculating the variance of these predictions
      • Or, can calculate it via matrix algebra as formula above shows

*Note: \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is a vector of regression coefficients and that the \(\tau\)’s are not included. \(\widehat{Var}(\mathbf{x})\) is the covariance matrix of the x variables

Potential drawbacks of coefficient interpretations

  • The metric of y* is determined mainly by the thresholds (\(\tau\))
    • Instead of assuming equidistant category spacing, the ordinal model spaces the outcome categories based on the \(\tau\) estimates
  • How do we estimate what the thresholds are?
    • To start, the only information we have is the proportion of responses in each outcome category (the Percent column of the pillokR frequency table above)

Estimating thresholds in the ordinal model

  • Recall that the ordinal model is a cumulative model in its design and coefficients. So, we examine the cumulative proportion in each category (proportion ≤ j; the Cum. column of the frequency table)
  • If we assume an underlying standardized normal distribution [N ~ (0,1)] for y*, convert the cumulative proportions to z-scores:
