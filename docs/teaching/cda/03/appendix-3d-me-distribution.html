Appendix 3D: Distribution of Marginal Effects

  • Recall that an average marginal effect (AME) is the mean (average) of separate marginal effects calculated for each observation
    • The difference between two predictions for each observation indicates the effect for that specific observation (MEi)

\[{ME}_{i} = \eta(x_{k} = {end}_{i}, \mathbf{x}_{-k} = \mathbf{x}_{-k,i}) - \eta(x_{k} = {start}_{i}, \mathbf{x}_{-k} = \mathbf{x}_{-k,i})\]

  • Effects will vary for each observation because start and end values will differ (e.g., \({start}_{i}\) = your current age; \({end}_{i}\) could be your current age + 1) and the models are inherently nonlinear
  • Effects will also vary because value of control variables (\(\mathbf{x}_{-k,i}\)) will also differ for each observation and the models are inherently interactive
  • The average marginal effect (AME) averages these effects over all observations in the data:

\[AME = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i = 1}^{N}\left\{\eta(x_{k} = {end}_{i}, \mathbf{x}_{-k} = \mathbf{x}_{-k,i}) - \eta(x_{k} = {start}_{i}, \mathbf{x}_{-k} = \mathbf{x}_{-k,i})\right\}\]

Examining distributions of MEs

  • Averages do not indicate variation in the sample.
    • E.g., Racial differences might depend on the levels of other variables
      • We already know from our marginsplot that the effect of BMI is different across the range of BMI—which means it varies for different people as people have different BMIs (due to nonlinearities in the effect of BMI)
      • Effect of BMI might also depend on other factors accounted for in the model (e.g., for men vs women; for people of different ages; etc.)
  • This suggests the importance of looking at the distribution of marginal effects
  • This is not commonly done, but should be (and is now easy to do in Stata 14+)
    • See Appendix 3A for details

library(haven)           # import Stata data and labels
library(dplyr)           # data manipulation
library(ggplot2)         # graphs
library(marginaleffects) # predictions and marginal effects
library(cleanplots)      # graph scheme
cleanplots_defaults()

hrs <- read_dta("https://tdmize.github.io/data/data/hrs06.dta")

# Treat binary/nominal variables as factors using their value labels
hrs <- hrs |>
  mutate(black = as_factor(black), woman = as_factor(woman), ed4cat = as_factor(ed4cat))

diabmod <- glm(diabetes ~ age + black + bmi + woman + ed4cat,
  family = binomial(link = "logit"), data = hrs)

# Marginal effects for every observation
MEs_black <- comparisons(diabmod, variables = "black")
MEs_bmi <- slopes(diabmod, variables = "bmi")

ggplot(MEs_bmi, aes(x = estimate)) +
  geom_histogram(aes(y = after_stat(100 * count / sum(count))), bins = 50,
    fill = NA, color = "black") +
  geom_vline(xintercept = mean(MEs_bmi$estimate), color = "red") +
  annotate("text", x = .012, y = 4.4, label = "AME = 0.015") +
  labs(title = "[3.14.a] Distribution of Marginal Effects of BMI on Pr(Diabetes)",
    x = "Marginal Effect of BMI", y = "Percent")

Comparison of distribution of marginal effects in linear and nonlinear models

  • What would you expect the distribution of marginal effects to look like if the model was linear and additive?
  • Consider BMI as the dependent variable now with two different specifications:
    • Continuous BMI (≈ 10 – 83)
    • Binary: “low or medium BMI=0” vs. “high BMI=1”
  • Our focal IVs are age and gender (coded as woman=1)
    • Also controlling for race and education
  • Stata
  • R

  • Remember that logit/probit models are fully interactive—so it can be informative to examine the distributions for multiple groups

  • R
