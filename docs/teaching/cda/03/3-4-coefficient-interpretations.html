3.4 Coefficient Interpretations

  • The binary logit model coefficients can be interpreted in terms of
    • y* metric (not covered here; see Appendix)
    • Log odds (rarely useful)
    • Odds ratios
      • Odds ratios are a common but often unsatisfactory method of interpretation

Model is linear in the log of the odds

  • The logit ≡ log of the odds

\[Odds = \Omega = \frac{\Pr(y = 1)}{\Pr(y = 0)}\]

  • The logit model is linear in the logit

\[\ln\left[\frac{\Pr(y = 1\,|\,\mathbf{x})}{1 - \Pr(y = 1\,|\,\mathbf{x})}\right] = \ln\Omega(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}\boldsymbol{\beta} = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x_{1} + \beta_{2}x_{2} + \beta_{3}x_{3}\]

For a unit change in xk, the logit is expected to change by βk, holding other variables constant

  • Substantively, what does a change of βk logits mean?
    • To understand the change in logit, we transform it into odds

Change logit to odds and compute odds ratio (ORs)

  • Take the exponential of the logit with a focus on x3:

\[\begin{aligned} \Omega(\mathbf{x}) &= \exp[\ln\Omega(\mathbf{x})] = \exp(\mathbf{x}\boldsymbol{\beta}) \\ &= e^{\beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x_{1} + \beta_{2}x_{2} + \beta_{3}x_{3}} \\ &= e^{\beta_{0}}e^{\beta_{1}x_{1}}e^{\beta_{2}x_{2}}{\color{red}{e^{\beta_{3}x_{3}}}} = \Omega(\mathbf{x}, x_{3}) \end{aligned}\]

  • Let x3 change by 1

\[\begin{aligned} \Omega(\mathbf{x}, x_{3} + 1) &= e^{\beta_{0}}e^{\beta_{1}x_{1}}e^{\beta_{2}x_{2}}{\color{green}{e^{\beta_{3}(x_{3} + 1)}}} \\ &= e^{\beta_{0}}e^{\beta_{1}x_{1}}e^{\beta_{2}x_{2}}{\color{red}{e^{\beta_{3}x_{3}}}}{\color{blue}{e^{\beta_{3}}}} \end{aligned}\]

  • The odds ratio

\[\frac{\text{Ending } \Omega}{\text{Starting } \Omega} = \frac{\Omega(\mathbf{x}, x_{3} + 1)}{\Omega(\mathbf{x}, x_{3})} = \frac{e^{\beta_{0}}e^{\beta_{1}x_{1}}e^{\beta_{2}x_{2}}{\color{red}{e^{\beta_{3}x_{3}}}}{\color{blue}{e^{\beta_{3}}}}}{e^{\beta_{0}}e^{\beta_{1}x_{1}}e^{\beta_{2}x_{2}}{\color{red}{e^{\beta_{3}x_{3}}}}} = {\color{blue}{e^{\beta_{3}}}}\]

For a unit increase in xk, the odds increase/decrease by a factor of OR holding other variables constant

Interpreting odds ratios (ORs)

  • OR is a multiplicative coefficient:

    For a unit increase in xk, the odds increase/decrease by a factor of OR holding other variables constant

    • 1 indicates no effect (e.g., 0.55 * 1 = 0.55)
    • For exp(βk)>1, the odds are exp(βk) times larger
      • Thus, a positive effect
    • For exp(βk)<1, the odds are exp(βk) times smaller
      • Thus, a negative effect

  • Magnitudes of positive and negative ORs are compared by taking the inverse of the negative effect (or vice versa)

    • A positive OR=2 (i.e., 2/1) has same magnitude as “negative” OR=0.5 (i.e., 1/2)
    • An OR=0.1 (i.e.,1/10) is much larger than OR=2 (i.e., 2/1)
      • The equivalent positive effect size is OR=10 (i.e., 10/1)

  • You should code your DV in the way that makes it more natural to interpret

    • E.g., You can model the odds of voting or the odds of not voting

Example of what not to do. If your research question is about vaccination rates, don’t code DV as not vaccinated

*Also use a normal y-axis label…

Examples odds ratio interpretations in binary logit

Example binary/nominal IV interpretations:

Conservative’s odds of supporting marijuana legalization are lower than non-conservative’s by a factor of 0.250, holding other variables constant

Hispanic adults have odds of supporting marijuana legalization that are lower than White adults’ by a factor of 0.289 (other variables held constant). Black and White adults odds of supporting marijuana legalization are not significantly different… [and so on for other categories]

Example continuous IV interpretations:

Controlling for other factors, a one-year increase in age decreases the odds of supporting marijuana legalization by a factor of 0.966

After accounting for other factors, family income is not associated with the odds of supporting marijuana legalization

Interpretation tip: A non-significant effect has a confidence interval that includes positive, null, and negative values. I do not think it is appropriate to interpret the direction of a null effect.

Considerations when interpreting odds ratios (ORs)

  • ORs are multiplicative effects—so you cannot say it “increases the odds by _____” (as you would with an additive effect)
    • Instead, you must specify that it increases or decreases the odds by a factor of/ # times which indicates effect is multiplicative instead of additive
  • You also cannot say things such as it “doubles the chance” of something happening or “makes it half as likely”—these are probability statements
    • Odds are not in the probability metric
    • You must interpret ORs in terms of what they suggest in terms of a multiplicative change in the odds
      • E.g., An OR=2 does not necessarily “double the chance” of observing an outcome
      • The substantive impact in terms of probability depends on what the starting value is (see the next few sections for examples)
  • In general, I do not find odds ratios interpretations very satisfying
    • Odds are less intuitive than probabilities; a ratio of two odds is much less intuitive than probabilities
    • The same size OR can have drastically different effects on the probability depending on the starting value

How OR’s and Pr(y) are related

  • The odds and probability are linked nonlinearly (see below)
  • The odds (often denoted \(\Omega\) [Omega]) are:
    • The probability of something happening (probability often denoted \(\pi\) [pi])
    • Compared to the probability of it not happening (1 – \(\pi\))

\[\Omega = \frac{\pi}{1 - \pi}\]

  • If we know the odds, can convert to probability:

\[\pi = \frac{\Omega}{1 + \Omega}\]

Effect sizes with multiplicative effects on the odds

  • Think about doubling the odds from 1/10 to 2/10
    • Probability of 0.091 becomes 0.167
      • An increase of 0.076
  • Think about doubling the odds from 1/1 to 2/1
    • Probability of 0.500 becomes 0.667
      • An increase of 0.167
  • Think about doubling the odds of winning the lottery from 1/100,000,000 to 2/100,000,000
    • Probability of 0.00000001 becomes 0.00000002
      • An increase of 0.00000001
  • The effect size depends on the starting probability
    • “Doubling the odds” means something fundamentally different across the two examples despite being the same multiplicative effect size

Interpretations in the natural metric

  • Many have argued that probabilities are the more “natural metric” to interpret binary outcomes (e.g., Mood 2010; Mize 2019; Long and Mustillo 2021)
    • The mean of a binary DV is the proportion; in model terms, this is a probability
    • E.g., 75% of the GSS sample supports marijuana legalization; the probability someone supports marijuana legalization is 0.75 (odds are 3/1)
  • Odds have no ceiling for positive effects, but negative effects are constrained from 0 to 1
    • Makes judging effect size difficult
  • Probabilities—and effects on the probabilities—are always between 0 and 1
    • Absolute value of a marginal effect on the probabilities demonstrates magnitude of effect size (e.g., -.05 and .05 same magnitude)
